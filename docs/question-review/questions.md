## READING: Panda Week (r-pandas, both, topic: Pandas)

### Text A: Panda Week is coming (Web page, Hollins Park Wildlife Centre website)

This summer, Hollins Park Wildlife Centre is holding its first ever Panda Week. For nine days, our two giant pandas, Juniper and Tao, will be the stars of the park. We think they are the most charming animals you will ever meet.

**About giant pandas**

**Tickets**

- Child (3 to 15): £9.00

Save 10% when you book online at least one day before your visit.

**Please remember**

- Pandas like a quiet space. Please keep your voice low on the Bamboo Walk.
- Flash photography is not allowed near the panda enclosure.

### Text B: We went to Panda Week. Was it worth it? (Blog post, The Weekend Wanderer blog)

Last Saturday, my partner, our two children and I spent the day at Hollins Park for Panda Week. I'll be honest: I wasn't sure a whole week about two animals would be worth the drive. I was wrong.

We arrived just before the 10:30 feeding talk, and it was the best part of the day. The keeper explained that bamboo gives pandas very little energy, so they have to eat a lot of it. My eight-year-old hasn't stopped talking about it since.

By the afternoon, things were less exciting. Tao was nowhere to be seen, and Juniper slept in the corner of the enclosure for the whole of the 14:00 question time. The Bamboo Walk was also very crowded, and we struggled to hear the keeper over the noise.

Booking online the day before saved us a few pounds on our family ticket, which helped, because the café prices were shocking: nearly £4 for a small juice.

My advice? Go early and take a packed lunch. The morning was magical. The afternoon was mostly a sleeping ball of fur.

### Text B: A month of eating (and waiting for buses) in Israel (Blog post, Slow Road travel blog)

Everyone told me the food would be good. Nobody told me it would ruin every other breakfast for me. Shakshuka, eggs cooked in a rich tomato and pepper sauce, is now my idea of the perfect start to the day.

Lunch was usually falafel stuffed into a warm pitta with salad and tahini, bought from a small stall. The best one I had was in a side street near the market in Tel Aviv, where the owner refused to let me leave without trying his hummus too.

### Text B: Behind the scenes at the Panda House (Magazine article, Marlow Wildlife Park members' magazine)

It took 14 months to build the new Panda House at Marlow Wildlife Park, and almost every detail was planned with the pandas in mind. Head keeper Priya Nair showed us around before the doors opened to the public.

**A home that feels like a forest**

Each panda has its own indoor room, which is kept cool all year, and a large outdoor garden. Giant pandas come from the mountain forests of south-west China, so the garden has hills to climb, trees for shade and a small stream. "Pandas are happiest when there is plenty to climb and somewhere shady to rest," says Priya.

**Bamboo, bamboo and more bamboo**

Bamboo makes up almost all of a giant panda's diet, but it gives very little energy, so pandas spend much of the day eating. The park grows some of its own bamboo. The rest arrives fresh by lorry every week from a specialist grower (you can see the grower's farm on the map on page 4). Keepers check every delivery by hand and throw away any stalks that look dry.

**A keeper's day**

Keepers start at 7:00 am by checking that both pandas are well. Next they clean the rooms and prepare the first feed. To help the pandas, China has set up protected nature reserves, where trees cannot be cut down. There are now dozens of reserves across the mountains, and together they protect large areas of forest. Many pandas now live in small patches of forest that are cut off from each other. **How can zoos help?**

Zoos cannot return pandas to the wild by themselves, but they can still make a difference. Money raised by zoos and their visitors helps to pay for the reserves and the rangers. Behind every successful conservation project is a team of skilled people, yet the sector is struggling to find them. The shortage is not new, but it is growing. Young people face real obstacles. Many entry-level jobs are seasonal and poorly paid, and competition for places on the best courses is fierce. Employers are keen to change that picture. Conservation is, after all, "the long game", and a single breeding programme can take a decade to show results. That is why organisations are offering more apprenticeships, short courses and paid trainee posts than ever before, and why many now accept volunteering as proof of ability.

> **Advice from the Wildlife Skills Council:** Thinking of a career in conservation? Start by volunteering locally. Ask about an apprenticeship rather than assuming you need a degree. Join a short course to build your confidence. And remember: you do not have to choose a path straight away.

The Dead Sea is one of the most unusual places in the world. It sits more than 400 metres below sea level, which makes it the lowest place on land on Earth, and its water is so salty that it holds your body up. Even people who cannot swim can lie back and float.

**How to get the most from your visit**

Local guide Noa Levi has taken hundreds of visitors to the Dead Sea, and she has some simple advice. "Walk in slowly, sit down in the water and then lean back," she says. "Never put your head under, and keep splashing to a minimum. Salt water in the eyes really stings."

Salt water also stings tiny cuts, so avoid shaving on the morning of your visit. Most people stay in the water for a short time (about 15 minutes is enough), because the salt can dry out your skin. Afterwards, rinse off in the fresh water showers on the beach, which also has changing rooms nearby.

**Mud and minerals**

Many visitors cover themselves in the dark mud from the shore. The mud is full of minerals, and it makes your skin feel wonderfully soft. Let it dry for a few minutes in the sun, then wash it off in the sea. Do not forget to take a camera, but keep it somewhere dry!

**Sun and water**

It can be extremely hot by the Dead Sea, even in the early morning. Wear a hat, put on plenty of sun cream and drink lots of fresh water. Wear sandals, too, because the stones on the shore can be sharp. Children should always stay close to an adult in the water.

Floating there is the most relaxing thing you can do on holiday, and it is an experience you will never forget.

### Text A: Easy shakshuka (Recipe web page, Everyday Kitchen website)

Shakshuka is a tasty breakfast dish from Israel. It is made with eggs cooked in a spicy tomato sauce.

You will need: **4 eggs**, **1 onion**, **2 red peppers**, **1 tin of tomatoes** and **1 teaspoon of cumin**.

Cook the chopped onion and peppers for ten minutes. Add the tomatoes and cumin, then cook for five more minutes. Next, crack the eggs into the sauce and cook until they are firm. Serve with fresh bread. It takes about thirty minutes to make, and it serves four people.

### Text B: Eating your way around the markets of Israel (Magazine article, Table & Trail food magazine)

If you want to understand a country, visit its food markets. In Israel, the biggest markets, such as the Carmel Market in Tel Aviv and the Mahane Yehuda Market in Jerusalem, are noisy, colourful places where stallholders shout out their prices and the smell of fresh bread fills the air. Markets are also where local people do their everyday shopping, not just tourists. Allow at least two hours to explore.

**What can you eat?**

Almost every market has a stall selling falafel, which are crispy balls made from chickpeas, usually served in warm pitta bread with salad. You will also find hummus, a smooth dip made from chickpeas, tahini (a paste made from sesame seeds) and lemon. Many cafés serve small dishes called "mezze", which are shared by everyone at the table. Fresh pitta is baked in the market every hour, so it is often still warm when you buy it.

**More than food**

Look out for the spice stalls, which sell bags of colourful spices piled high in sacks. There are also stalls selling fresh fruit and vegetables, olives, cheese, dried fruit and nuts. Many vendors are happy to let customers taste before they buy. Some markets also have musicians, so you can enjoy a tune while you shop.

**When should you go?**

Friday is the busiest day. Many shops close for Shabbat, the day of rest, which begins on Friday evening, so people shop for the weekend before it starts. Food writer Yael Cohen advises going in the morning: "The crowds are smaller and the fruit and vegetables are at their freshest."

Whatever time you go, bring cash and wear comfortable shoes. The falafel at the Carmel Market is the best in the world, and you will fall in love with the food.

## READING: The future of the village (rl2-geography, L2, topic: UK geography)

### Text A: Moving to a village: what to consider (Web page, Fenland Rural Housing Advice) [paragraphs numbered]

[1] Many people dream of swapping the city for a village, with its open views, quiet lanes and friendly faces. Village life can be wonderful, but it works best for those who have thought carefully about what they are choosing. A little research now will help you avoid disappointment later.

[2] Think first about getting around. Many villages have only a few buses each day, and some have none at all in the evening, so a car is often essential. If you do not drive, check the timetable before you commit, because a short journey can turn into a bit of a trek.

[3] Next, look at local services. A village shop with a post office, a primary school and a doctor's surgery can make daily life much easier, but each of these is hard to keep open when the population is small. Find out which facilities are within walking distance and which are a drive away.

[4] Housing is the next big question. Homes in villages are often older, with larger gardens, and they can cost more than similar properties in towns because so few come up for sale. Rented homes are scarce, so you may have to wait. Ask the parish council whether any new houses are planned.

[5] Community matters too. Villages thrive when people take part, whether by helping at the summer fair, joining the church committee or simply popping into the pub. Why not go along to an event before you move? You will soon get a feel for the place.

[6] Finally, visit at different times of year. A village in June is a picture postcard, but in January it can feel very different. Spend a weekend there in winter if you can, and make sure you are happy with what you find. If you are, you're all set.

### Text B: Why are young people leaving our villages? (Magazine article, Rural Review magazine)

Walk through almost any English village on a weekday afternoon and you will notice who is missing. A survey of 2,500 people aged 18 to 30 by the Rural Futures Group found that 57 per cent of those raised in villages had moved away by the age of 25, and only one in eight of them expected to return.

The reasons are not mysterious. House prices in villages are often far higher than local wages can support, and homes to rent are rare. For many young people, a village home is not so much the first rung of the ladder as "a ladder with the bottom rungs missing". Others leave for work, since jobs in the countryside tend to be scarce, seasonal or poorly paid.

Transport is another barrier. Without a car, a young person who lives in a village may depend on a bus that runs twice a day, which makes college, evening jobs and friendships difficult to sustain. Some say the situation leaves them feeling cut off, and no wonder.

The effect on the villages themselves can be severe. As young families leave, schools lose pupils and shops lose customers. Eventually, the village risks "a hollowing out": the buildings remain, but the life drains away. Rural pubs, post offices and clubs are closing at a worrying rate.

> **Advice from the Rural Communities Forum:** Parish councils should survey local young people about what they need. Landowners should consider selling small plots for affordable homes. Bus operators should trial evening and weekend routes. And villagers should welcome newcomers, whatever their age.

There are encouraging examples. In one village, a community land trust has built eight low-cost homes, all reserved for local people, and the primary school has since gained a dozen pupils. Residents say that the change has brought new energy to the place.

In my view, a village without young people is a village without a future, and it is the duty of everyone who lives there to do something about it. Waiting for someone else to act is not an option.

### Text C: Speech to a parish council meeting (Transcript of a speech, Parish council meeting)

Good evening, everyone, and thank you for coming to what I know is a difficult discussion. I have lived in this village for forty years, I have served on this council for twelve, and I am asking you tonight to say yes to the new homes on Mill Field.

Let me begin with a story. Last spring, a young woman I know, born and raised in this parish, told me she was leaving. She had a job at the surgery, a family nearby and a love for this place, but she could not find a home she could afford. When she left, we did not just lose a neighbour. We lost a future.

So what do we need? First, we need homes that local people can afford. Second, we need young families, because without them the school will close. Third, we need to keep our shop, our hall and our pub alive, and they can only survive if people live here all year round.

Will there be disruption? Of course there will. Will some of you miss the view across the field? I will, too. But what is the alternative? A village of empty houses, shuttered windows and a bus stop that nobody uses. I refuse to let that happen on my watch.

The figures speak for themselves. Eighteen of the twenty-four new homes will be reserved for people with a local connection, and the developer has promised to pay for a new footpath, a play area and a bus shelter. Since our school roll fell by a third in five years, we cannot afford to hesitate.

I have heard the worry about traffic. It is a fair worry, and I share it. That is why I have asked for a speed limit on the lane and a crossing by the school. We can protect our village and still make room for the next generation.

Think of the Saturday fair in ten years' time. I picture young families with pushchairs, children running between the stalls and every volunteer wearing a smile. That is the village I want to leave behind, and I believe it is the village you want, too.

It will be difficult, but it will be worth it. Please vote with your hearts, and vote for the future. Thank you.

## Fix it: Fix the apostrophes (fix-apostrophes)

1. Im going to the library after college. → I'm going to the library after college. (I'm is short for I am. The apostrophe shows the missing letter.)
2. We couldnt find the platform. → We couldn't find the platform. (Couldn't is short for could not.)
3. The dogs lead was too short. → The dog's lead was too short. (One dog owns the lead, so the apostrophe goes before the s.)
4. The teachers meeting starts at four. → The teachers' meeting starts at four. (Several teachers own the meeting, so the apostrophe goes after the s.)
5. Its a lovely day and the sun has got its hat on. → It's a lovely day and the sun has got its hat on. (It's means it is. Its (no apostrophe) shows belonging. Only the first one needed fixing.)
6. Three bus's were late this morning. → Three buses were late this morning. (A plural does not take an apostrophe.)
7. The childrens playground is closed. → The children's playground is closed. (Children is already plural, so add 's.)
8. Theyre not sure whose coming. → They're not sure who's coming. (They're is they are, and who's is who is. Whose shows belonging.)

## Fix it: Fix the capital letters and full stops (fix-capitals)

1. on monday i went to london with my sister. → On Monday I went to London with my sister. (Days, places and the word I all start with a capital letter, and so does the first word.)
2. We waited for the train it was late. → We waited for the train. It was late. (Two separate ideas need a full stop between them.)
3. my favourite subject is english, and my teacher is mrs patel. → My favourite subject is English, and my teacher is Mrs Patel. (Names of subjects such as English, titles and names need capital letters.)
4. The cafe was busy. so we sat outside. → The cafe was busy, so we sat outside. (A sentence should not start with so after a full stop. A comma joins the two parts.)
5. Are you coming to the match on saturday → Are you coming to the match on Saturday? (A question ends with a question mark, and days start with a capital letter.)
6. What a brilliant goal → What a brilliant goal! (An exclamation takes an exclamation mark.)
7. In december we visited the Dead Sea in israel. → In December we visited the Dead Sea in Israel. (Months and countries start with a capital letter.)
8. The panda eats bamboo. it can eat for twelve hours a day. → The panda eats bamboo. It can eat for twelve hours a day. (The first word after a full stop needs a capital letter.)

## Fix it: Fix the commas (fix-commas)

1. We bought bread milk eggs and cheese. → We bought bread, milk, eggs and cheese. (Commas separate items in a list.)
2. However the train was late. → However, the train was late. (A comma follows an opening word such as however.)
3. When we arrived at the station we bought our tickets. → When we arrived at the station, we bought our tickets. (A comma follows an opening clause.)
4. My brother who lives in Leeds is a nurse. → My brother, who lives in Leeds, is a nurse. (Commas go round extra information.)
5. I wanted to go but it was raining so I stayed in. → I wanted to go, but it was raining, so I stayed in. (A comma goes before but and so when they join two full ideas.)
6. The tour was long, the guide was excellent. → The tour was long. The guide was excellent. (A comma cannot join two complete sentences. Use a full stop (or a joining word).)
7. I am writing to ask about the trip, thank you. → I am writing to ask about the trip. Thank you. (A comma cannot join two separate sentences. Use a full stop.)
8. If you are unsure ask a member of staff. → If you are unsure, ask a member of staff. (A comma follows an opening if clause.)

## Fix it: Fix the spelling (fix-spelling)

1. There going to be late. → They're going to be late. (They're means they are. There points to a place.)
2. I would like to here your opinion. → I would like to hear your opinion. (Hear is what you do with your ears. Here is a place.)
3. It was to cold to go outside. → It was too cold to go outside. (Too means more than needed or also.)
4. We went to the park for our brake. → We went to the park for our break. (A break is a rest. A brake stops a bike.)
5. The weather was nice, accept for the wind. → The weather was nice, except for the wind. (Except means apart from. Accept means to take.)
6. I recieved your letter yesterday. → I received your letter yesterday. (I before E, except after C.)
7. The new libary opens on Monday. → The new library opens on Monday. (Say it slowly: lie-brar-y.)
8. This is definately the best answer. → This is definitely the best answer. (Definitely has the word finite in the middle.)

## Fix it: Fix the grammar (fix-grammar)

1. There is three buses to the town. → There are three buses to the town. (Three buses is plural, so use are.)
2. Each of the students have a ticket. → Each of the students has a ticket. (Each is singular, so it goes with has.)
3. Yesterday we go to the museum. → Yesterday we went to the museum. (Yesterday means the past, so use the past tense.)
4. She don't like crowds. → She doesn't like crowds. (She goes with doesn't.)
5. I seen the film last week. → I saw the film last week. (Saw is the past tense. Seen needs have.)
6. We was waiting for an hour. → We were waiting for an hour. (We goes with were.)
7. Me and my friend went to the match. → My friend and I went to the match. (Use I when you are doing the action, and put yourself last.)
8. It was more better than I expected. → It was better than I expected. (Better already means more good, so do not add more.)
